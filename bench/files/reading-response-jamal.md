# Reading Response: Of Mice and Men, Chapters 1 to 3

Jamal Pierce, English 10

## My main idea

After three chapters I think the book is about **who gets to have a dream**. George and Lennie have one, but the book keeps showing us that people like them are not really supposed to. Slim, Candy and even Curley all have their own reasons for being on the ranch, and none of them seems to have a place that is truly theirs.

## What I noticed

- George says he could "get along so easy" without Lennie, but he never leaves. That tells me he is complaining to feel better, not because he means it.
- Lennie remembers the dream better than George does, even though he forgets almost everything else. The dream is the only thing he really holds on to.
- Curley is small and picks fights with bigger men. I think Steinbeck wants us to see him as insecure and dangerous at the same time.
- Candy's dog is shot in chapter 3. Carlson says that the dog is "no good to himself," and everyone goes along with it. Later Candy says he should have done it himself.

## The scene I keep thinking about

The shooting of Candy's dog is the one that stays with me. At first it seems like a side story. Carlson is annoyed by the smell and the old dog is in pain, so the shooting looks practical. But Candy's reaction shows how powerless he is. He does not argue back very hard, and he later regrets it. I think the scene is a warning. If a ranch hand who is old and useless can be disposed of, then what about Lennie, who is strong but not smart? Slim says to George, "You hadda, George," and later we hear something similar. I get the feeling that Steinbeck is setting up this idea early so that we are not completely surprised later.

## A question for class

Is the dream real, or is it a story they tell to get through the day? When Candy offers his savings, the dream suddenly looks possible, and that makes me more nervous, not less. Maybe the closer a dream gets, the more it can be lost.
