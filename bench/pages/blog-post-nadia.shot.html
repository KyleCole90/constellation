George, Lennie, and the Dream That Isn’t There

Posted by Nadia R. · Reading response: Of Mice and Men, chapters 1–3

I have a confession. The first time George told Lennie about the farm, I believed it. The rabbits, the garden, the little house where they would “live off the fatta the lan’.” I wanted it for them. It took me until the middle of chapter 3 to realize that I was falling for the same story Lennie was.

What I notice is that George never tells the dream the same way twice when someone else is in the room. With Lennie, he tells it like a lullaby. With Slim, he sounds almost embarrassed. That difference seems to matter. The dream is a private thing, and saying it out loud to a stranger is risky because the stranger might laugh.

“Guys like us, that work on ranches, are the loneliest guys in the world.”

That line from chapter 1 is where I think Steinbeck tells us what the book is about. It isn’t really about the farm. It’s about what people will believe, and what they will carry around, to avoid being alone.

Here is where I disagree with the way we discussed it in class. Mr. Lindgren said that George stays with Lennie out of duty. I think it’s the other way around: Lennie keeps George from becoming the lonely ranch hand he describes. Lennie needs George to survive, but George needs Lennie to have a reason to hope.

The scene with Candy’s dog in chapter 3 makes this clearer, and I will write about it in my next post, because it deserves its own space. For now, I’ll say that I closed the book feeling worried, and I don’t think Steinbeck wants us to feel better.

Questions I still have: Why does Curley’s wife not get a name? Is the dream a plan or a prayer? And why does Slim, who is so wise, never seem surprised by anything?
