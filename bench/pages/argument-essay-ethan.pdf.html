Pay Before You Owe: Why Every Student Should Graduate Knowing How Money Works

Most students leave high school able to find the area of a triangle and unable to say what happens when they only pay the minimum on a credit card. I did not realize how large that gap was until my older cousin, a college sophomore, showed me a statement with $1,900 in balance and $41 in interest charged in a single month. He had no idea how it happened. Nobody had ever told him. Because personal finance shapes adult life more than almost any other subject, our school should require a semester of financial literacy for graduation.

The need is easy to document. A 2022 national survey of young adults reported by the Hartwell Institute found that fewer than half could correctly answer a basic question about compound interest (Okafor 14). Those who could not were far more likely to carry high-interest debt. That finding is not a surprise. The financial products young people meet at eighteen, from student loans to buy-now-pay-later apps, are designed by professionals and described in fine print. Expecting a teenager to navigate them without instruction is like handing someone car keys without a driving lesson.

In this essay I will argue that a required course would be effective, affordable and fair. I will first show that students who take such courses make measurably better decisions, then explain how our school could add one without cutting anything vital, and finally answer the objection that parents, not schools, should teach money.

Park 2

The strongest evidence that the course works comes from states that already require it. Researchers at the Lindqvist Center tracked graduates of five states with mandatory courses and compared them with graduates of states without one. Five years later, the first group had credit scores about 15 points higher and were about a fifth less likely to have an account in default (Lindqvist and Reyes 31). The effect was largest among students from lower-income families, which matters because those students are least likely to have a relative who can explain a loan.

The course also changes behavior early. A teacher in Ohio whom the Hartwell Institute interviewed said that after the unit on budgeting, students began asking whether their part-time wages were being taxed correctly, and several noticed errors on their own pay stubs (Okafor 22). Learning to read a pay stub or a lease is not abstract. It is the kind of knowledge that protects a person on the first day of their first job.

Some people will say that the benefits come from the students who choose such courses, not from the courses themselves. That is a fair worry, and it is why the mandatory states are the better comparison: nobody chose. When everyone takes the class, the improvement still appears. That does not prove causation beyond doubt, but it makes the selection explanation much weaker.

A skeptic could still ask whether students remember what they learn. Here too the evidence is encouraging. Courses that use simulations, such as managing a pretend paycheck for a month, produced better retention than lecture-only courses (Lindqvist and Reyes 38). That suggests the right design, not just the right topic.

The second part of my argument is practical. Our school day already has room for this course. Students are required to take one semester of health and one of a elective in the second year. A half-credit course in personal finance could replace the elective slot for sophomores, or share the health slot in a split-year schedule. No teacher would need to be hired for it: three members of our math and business departments already hold the certification, according to our guidance counselor, Mr. Duarte.

Cost is also small. The free curriculum offered by several national nonprofits includes lesson plans, simulation software and assessments. The only expense is a few hours of teacher training. Compare that to the cost of one student defaulting on a loan, which can follow them for a decade.

The course need not be heavy. A good semester can cover four units: budgeting and paychecks, credit and debt, saving and investing, and consumer protection. Each unit ends in a project students can use. For example, the debt unit asks them to compare three real credit card offers and calculate what it costs to pay each off over a year. Students leave with a skill and a finished product, not just a quiz grade.

Some will worry that a new requirement crowds out other classes. But graduation requirements already change when the world changes; computer science became a requirement in my district only six years ago. Money is no less central to adult life than code.

Park 4

The strongest objection is that families, not schools, should teach children about money. I agree that parents matter, and a course can invite them in. Our district could send home a short guide with each unit so families can talk about their own choices. But the reality is that many households do not discuss money, and some parents never learned the basics themselves. If the school waits for every family to teach it, the students who most need it will be the ones who never get it.

A related worry is that a school course might push a single political view about debt or investing. That is manageable. The curriculum I am recommending teaches skills with a right answer, such as how compound interest works, how to read a loan agreement and how to compare fees. Where reasonable people disagree, for example about whether to rent or buy, the course can say so and let students weigh both.

In the end the question is simple. We already require students to learn the quadratic formula and the causes of the Civil War, because we decided those matter to a good life and an informed citizenry. If we are honest about what trips up young adults, a basic understanding of money belongs on that list. My cousin is smart, and he still learned about interest the hard way. Our school can make sure we do not.
