Marcus Teller · U.S. History, Period 6 · DBQ: The Gilded Age

Industrialization and the American Worker, 1865–1900

Prompt: Evaluate the extent to which industrialization improved the lives of American workers between 1865 and 1900.

When the Civil War ended in 1865, most Americans still worked on farms or in small shops. By 1900 the United States produced more steel than Britain and Germany combined, its cities were crowded with factories, and millions of workers, many of them immigrants, depended on a wage. Industrialization improved the lives of American workers in some ways, such as higher real wages and cheaper goods, but those improvements came at the cost of long hours, unsafe workplaces and the loss of independence. Because workers themselves had to fight for protections and employers and government often opposed them, industrialization improved workers’ lives only to a moderate extent.

The best evidence for improvement is economic. Document A, a table of average annual earnings, shows that nonfarm wages rose from about $380 in 1870 to about $490 in 1900. Because prices fell during much of this period, workers’ purchasing power grew even faster than wages. A household could afford manufactured clothing, tools and canned food that earlier generations had made or done without.

Still, Document A has limits. It reports an average, which hides large differences between a skilled machinist and an unskilled laborer, and it counts only wage earners, not the unemployed. The late nineteenth century was also marked by sharp depressions, in 1873 and 1893, when many workers lost all income. A wage that looks adequate on paper could vanish in a bad year.

Document B, an excerpt from Andrew Carnegie’s “Gospel of Wealth” (1889), offers a different argument. Carnegie claims that the gap between rich and poor is the “price” of civilization and that wealthy men should give their surplus back to the public through libraries and universities. It is important to consider his purpose. Carnegie wrote as a steel magnate defending the system that made him rich, and his essay is addressed to other wealthy men, not to workers. He does not mention the hours or wages of the men in his own mills. Therefore the document tells us more about how elites justified inequality than about what workers experienced.

Document C, a Lewis Hine photograph of young girls at spinning frames in a Southern mill, provides direct evidence of hardship. The children are small enough that the machines seem to tower over them, and some have no shoes. Hine took the photograph in 1908, slightly after our period, as part of his work for the National Child Labor Committee, which wanted to change laws. His purpose was to persuade, so he chose the most striking images. Even so, the census of 1900 recorded about 1.75 million children aged ten to fifteen working for pay, which suggests that Hine showed something common and not rare.

Document D, a circular issued by the Knights of Labor in 1886, calls on workers to demand an eight-hour day, the so-called “eight hours for work, eight hours for rest, eight hours for what we will.” The very fact that this was a demand tells us that a ten- or twelve-hour day was the norm. The circular also shows workers organizing across skill levels, which was unusual. Its limitation is that it comes from a union and so presents workers’ grievances in the strongest terms. Yet it is consistent with what historians know about factory hours at the time, which gives it credibility.

Outside evidence strengthens the case that the gains were limited. In the Haymarket affair of 1886, a rally for the eight-hour day in Chicago ended with a bomb and the arrest of labor leaders, and public opinion turned against unions for years. At Homestead in 1892, Carnegie Steel cut wages, locked out its workers and used Pinkerton detectives, and the state militia protected strikebreakers. In 1894, President Cleveland sent federal troops to end the Pullman Strike. In each case, the government helped employers, which supports the argument that workers could not rely on it to protect their interests.

On the other hand, the story is not entirely bleak. The Triangle fire was still to come in 1911, but earlier tragedies, such as railroad accidents and mine disasters, helped build pressure for the first state safety laws. The American Federation of Labor, founded in 1886 and led by Samuel Gompers, focused on practical goals like wages and hours, and by 1900 it had around half a million members. Skilled workers who joined these unions did win better pay and shorter hours. The labor movement was therefore one of the outcomes of industrialization that improved workers’ lives, even if it began as a response to harm.

There is also an argument that industrial cities gave immigrants and former farmers opportunities that were not available in the countryside. A mill worker in Lowell or a steelworker in Pittsburgh could earn more than a Sicilian peasant or a Southern sharecropper. For many families, this made the move worthwhile. But these gains were uneven. African Americans were mostly kept out of good industrial jobs, and women and children were paid much less than men for similar work.

In conclusion, industrialization improved American workers’ lives to a moderate extent. It raised average real wages and lowered prices, but it also produced dangerous workplaces, child labor, long hours and recurring unemployment. The documents show a split between those who profited from the system, like Carnegie, and those who bore its costs, like the children in Hine’s photograph. Perhaps the most important change was not material but political: workers learned that, to share in the nation’s new wealth, they would have to organize for it.
