# DBQ Response: Industrialization and American Workers

Elena Kowalski, U.S. History, Period 2

## Thesis

Industrialization improved the lives of American workers between 1865 and 1900 in a limited and uneven way. It raised wages and lowered the price of goods, but it also created dangerous workplaces, long hours and a power imbalance that workers could only challenge by organizing.

## Documents

Document A, the table of average earnings, shows that wages for nonfarm workers climbed from about $380 in 1870 to about $490 in 1900. This supports the idea that industrial jobs paid better than the farm labor many workers left behind. However, the table gives an average, so it hides the lowest paid workers. It also says nothing about how many hours were needed to earn those wages.

Document B is Andrew Carnegie's "Gospel of Wealth." Carnegie argues that wealth should be put to public use, such as libraries. His point of view is that of a rich employer, so he has a reason to present inequality as a good thing. He does not discuss his own workers, which is a significant gap.

Document C is a photograph of child laborers taken by Lewis Hine. It provides vivid evidence of harm, but Hine took the photograph in order to persuade people to pass child labor laws, so it shows the worst cases. Document D is a Knights of Labor circular demanding an eight-hour day. It shows that workers were organizing and that workdays were much longer than eight hours.

## Outside evidence

- The Homestead Strike (1892): Carnegie Steel cut wages and brought in Pinkerton guards. The union lost, and the company gained control over its workforce.
- The Pullman Strike (1894): federal troops ended the strike, which showed that the government often sided with employers.
- The American Federation of Labor (founded 1886): a union of skilled workers that focused on wages and hours, and it did win better conditions for its members.

## Analysis

Taken together, the documents show two sides of the same system. The wage data and Carnegie's philanthropy show benefits that were real, while the Hine photograph and the Knights of Labor circular show the cost. Most of the benefits went to people with power or skills. Unskilled workers, women and children had the least protection. Skilled workers in unions did better because they could bargain together.

The outside evidence adds that workers did not simply receive improvements, because they had to fight for them, and they often lost. That is the main reason I conclude that industrialization improved workers' lives only to a limited extent.

## Conclusion

Industrialization raised the standard of living for many Americans, but it also made life harder and less safe for others. The labor movement was the most important response, and it shaped the reforms of the Progressive Era that came next.
