Let Them Ride: Our City Should Give Students Free Bus Passes

Hannah Bell · English 10 · Argumentative Essay

Every school morning at 6:52, I watch the same thing at the corner of Dunmore and 5th. Four or five students stand at the stop, checking their phones and counting coins. Some pay $1.75 and some do not, because they cannot. A few will walk the two miles to school instead, and a few will not come at all. If a student cannot get to school, they cannot learn, and if the city has a bus passing right by their door, the fare should not be the thing standing between them and a classroom.

Our city should provide free bus passes to every public school student under eighteen. This policy would raise attendance, ease the burden on families, reduce traffic around schools, and cost far less than most people assume.

The Problem Is Not Small

At a student fare of $1.75 each way, a round trip costs $3.50. Over a 180-day school year that is $630, which does not count weekends, jobs or after-school activities. For a family with two children in high school, that is $1,260 a year, about the same as a month of rent for many households in our city. Transportation is not an extra. It is the first requirement of attending school.

The consequences show up in attendance. In the district’s own 2023 report, students who lived more than a mile from school and relied on public transit missed an average of 14 days per year, compared with 8 for those who were driven or lived closer. Chronic absence, defined as missing ten percent of the school year, was concentrated in the neighborhoods with the fewest cars (Marsh 6).

What Happens When Cities Try It

This is not a theory. Several cities have given students free rides, and the results are measurable. In one mid-sized city that began a free student pass in 2019, ridership by students rose 31 percent within a year. A follow-up survey of those students found that about a third said the pass was the reason they could participate in clubs, sports or tutoring, because they no longer had to choose between a late bus fare and a ride home (Marsh 11).

Measure Before free pass One year after
Students riding the bus daily 2,140 2,810
Average absences per student 11.2 days 9.6 days
Students in after-school programs 3,050 3,610

Table 1. Figures from the pilot city’s transit agency report (Okonjo 4).

The numbers are not magic, and I do not claim the pass alone caused every change. But three measures moved in the right direction at once, and the biggest gains came among families with the lowest incomes. That is what we would expect if cost was a real barrier.

Benefits Beyond the Bus

Free passes help the city, too. Every student on a bus is not in a parent’s car at 7:45 a.m., when the roads around our high school clog until the first bell. The transit agency told the pilot city’s council that peak-hour car trips around schools fell by an estimated 9 percent (Okonjo 7). Fewer cars means less pollution and fewer near-misses at crosswalks.

There is also a longer-term gain. Students who learn to use public transit at fourteen are more likely to use it as adults. A city that wants fuller buses in 2035 should start by getting teenagers comfortable on them now.

The Cost Objection

The most common objection is cost, and it deserves a straight answer. Our district enrolls about 18,500 students. If every one of them rode every school day at full price, the lost fare revenue would be roughly $11.6 million. But that scenario is unrealistic. Many students already walk, bike or are driven, and the transit agency estimates that only about a third of students ride regularly. Based on that, the true cost is closer to $3.9 million a year, and part of it is recovered when a free pass shifts students who would otherwise be driven onto buses that already run.

For perspective, our city’s annual transit budget is over $210 million, so the free pass would be under two percent. Cities already spend money on school buses, crossing guards and road repairs around campuses. Treating the public bus as another piece of school infrastructure is not radical.

Will It Be Abused?

Some people worry that free passes will let students ride all day instead of attending class. The pilot city handled this by limiting passes to student ID cards and by issuing them annually. There is no evidence in the agency’s report that it increased truancy; absence fell. Teenagers who want to skip class do not need a free bus to do it. The ones who benefit most are the ones who want to be at school and cannot afford to get there.

A second worry is overcrowding at peak hours. This is real, and the city should expect to add a handful of runs on the busiest school routes. The pilot city did this and funded it from the money saved on school-area traffic enforcement. A policy that costs a few extra buses is a good sign, not a bad one, because it means students are riding.

Conclusion

When we ask whether to spend public money, we usually ask what we get for it. Here we get higher attendance, calmer mornings on busy roads, more students in after-school programs, and a generation that sees the city as theirs. We also lift a cost off families who already do the hardest budgeting in the city. Our city council meets on the second Tuesday of next month. I hope that students, parents and teachers will go and ask for a free student pass. The bus is already running. We should let students on.
