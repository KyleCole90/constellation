Effect of Temperature on Catalase Activity in Chicken Liver

Wen Zhao · Biology, Period 4 · Lab date: October 9 · Lab partner: Rosalind Ortega

Introduction

Catalase is an enzyme found in the peroxisomes of nearly all living cells. Its job is to break down hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), a toxic by-product of metabolism, into water and oxygen gas: 2 H2O2 → 2 H2O + O2. Because oxygen is released as bubbles, the rate of the reaction can be estimated by measuring how much foam forms when catalase meets hydrogen peroxide.

Like all enzymes, catalase is a protein whose function depends on its three-dimensional shape. Heat increases the kinetic energy of molecules, so collisions between enzyme and substrate become more frequent and the reaction speeds up. At high enough temperatures, however, the bonds that hold the protein in shape break, and the active site no longer fits the substrate. This is called denaturation, and it is not reversible.

Question and Hypothesis

Question. How does the temperature of the reaction mixture affect the rate at which catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide?

Hypothesis. If the temperature of the hydrogen peroxide is raised from 5 °C toward 37 °C, then catalase activity will increase, because faster molecular motion produces more enzyme-substrate collisions. If the temperature is raised to 60 °C, activity will fall sharply, because the enzyme will begin to denature. I predict the peak will be near 37 °C, since the liver came from an animal whose body temperature is close to that value.

Materials

Chicken liver (fresh, cut into 1 cm cubes), 3% hydrogen peroxide, 16 test tubes (18 mm), four water baths (5, 20, 37 and 60 °C), thermometer, metric ruler, graduated cylinder (10 mL), forceps, timer, safety goggles.

Procedure

1. Four water baths were prepared and checked with a thermometer. Ice water gave 5 °C; the others were tap water or a hot plate adjusted to 20, 37 and 60 °C.

2. For each trial, 5.0 mL of 3% hydrogen peroxide was poured into a test tube and held in its water bath for five minutes so that the solution reached the bath temperature.

3. A 1 cm cube of liver was cut with a scalpel on a paper plate, picked up with forceps and dropped into the tube. The timer was started as the liver hit the solution.

4. After exactly 60 seconds, the height of the foam above the liquid was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm with a ruler held against the tube.

5. Each temperature was tested three times with a fresh cube of liver and fresh peroxide. The independent variable was temperature. The dependent variable was foam height. Liver size, peroxide concentration and volume, test tube diameter and reaction time were held constant.

Data

5 1.0 1.3 1.3 1.2 Slow, few small bubbles
20 3.1 3.6 4.7 3.8 Steady foaming

Analysis

Figure 1. Mean foam height at each temperature. Error bars show the range of the three trials.

Foam height rose from 1.2 cm at 5 °C to 5.9 cm at 37 °C, nearly a fivefold increase, and then collapsed to 0.4 cm at 60 °C. The 37 °C trials were the most consistent (range 0.7 cm). The 20 °C trials varied most (range 1.6 cm), which suggests that one cube of liver may have been larger than the others.

Conclusion and Error

The data support the hypothesis. Catalase activity increased with temperature up to 37 °C and dropped almost to zero at 60 °C, consistent with denaturation: the liver visibly changed color and the foam stopped. The optimum for this enzyme in this experiment therefore lies between 20 and 60 °C, probably close to 37 °C, although we did not test enough temperatures to locate it exactly.

Sources of error include liver cubes cut by hand, which differ in surface area and so in how much enzyme is exposed. Foam height is also an indirect measure of reaction rate, and tall foam can collapse or spill. A better design would mass each liver piece, collect the oxygen in an inverted graduated cylinder, and add trials at 30, 45 and 50 °C. Even so, the pattern is clear enough to show why a fever above about 41 °C is dangerous: human enzymes are tuned to a narrow temperature range.
